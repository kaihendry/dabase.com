AI agents can keep working while we are away from the keyboard. What does that mean for our time, our independence and the systems that still have to ship the code?

In episode 44 of AI Infrastructure, Kai Hendry and Vincent De Smet compare Orca, Claude remote control and Hermes reviews, and take a look at stacked pull requests. We debate Omarchy and open-source politics, the cost of frontier models versus local inference, and why agents still need a responsible human.

We also discuss helping the next generation learn engineering through product ownership, whether specialist agent roles help, and why CI keeps becoming a bottleneck. Along the way: Backstage and MCP, Spotify’s agent adoption, and collaborative feedback on AI artifacts.

A wide-ranging conversation about always-on AI agents and how they change daily work, developer workflows and infrastructure bottlenecks. Kai Hendry and Vincent De Smet trade practical notes on Orca, Claude Code, Codex and Hermes, compare model behaviour, and explain how agent automation reshapes code review, pull requests and CI. They also discuss Omarchy and open-source politics, hardware and hosting costs, access to frontier models, and the engineering judgement needed to maintain agent-produced code.

### [00:00](https://youtu.be/PXHpssCyRzs?t=0) Personal agent workflows and model mix

The hosts start with contrasting approaches to switching off: Vincent tries to keep weekends free of work, while Kai uses agents throughout the day. Kai describes using Orca from his phone and another laptop, with OpenAI’s Astra 6 helping improve his existing projects. They discuss the practical trade-offs of always-on machines, subscriptions and API keys, and the value of using more than one model ecosystem. Remote access makes it easier to keep work moving, but also harder to establish boundaries.

### [06:23](https://youtu.be/PXHpssCyRzs?t=383) Stacked PRs and agentic code review

Vincent demonstrates stacked pull requests: dependent changes are organised into a sequence that reviewers can examine and merge in order. He has added support to Hermes so each PR is reviewed against the appropriate point in the stack. They compare this with asking Opus to split work into smaller commits, debating whether the commit or the PR is the useful unit of review. Kai worries that several stacked PRs could multiply the already lengthy descriptions generated by AI. The discussion moves on to remote review, Git tools and out-of-hours notifications.

### [17:09](https://youtu.be/PXHpssCyRzs?t=1029) Omarchy, Linux on laptops and community politics

A long segment covers Omarchy, the DHH-led Arch Linux setup, as a practical route to a usable Linux desktop. Technical praise for installation and defaults sits alongside frustration with Mac hardware costs and macOS behaviour. The hosts also debate the political arguments surrounding open-source maintainers and donor-backed foundations, asking how much a founder’s views should influence a decision to use their software.
